MFVI and variational distributions


Mean Field Variational Inference (MFVI) refers to variational optimization that adopts the Mean Field (MF) assumption that the joint posterior of the parameters is factorizable along a specified partition (i.e. that some partition of the parameters leads to independence between the partition's subsets), thus leading to simpler posteriors that are much easier to sample from and making computation tractable and highly scalable.  Even though the true joint posterior may be highly dependent, the individual variational posterior distributions remain independent; variational bayes procedures for this reason are known to underestimate variance, essentially approximating a highly correlated posterior with a product of independent "bubbles."

"The variational distribution will typically "under-fit" the true posterior, often becoming overly certain (picking the largest axis-aligned blob it can fit inside the true posterior)"
Christopher Bishop’s "Pattern Recognition and Machine Learning" (PRML), specifically in Section 10.1.2.


However, the variational approximation to the posterior remains useful.  Though the individual variational posteriors do not capture dependence in the joint posterior, the variational distributions' parameters are optimized \textit{jointly} to minimize the KL divergence to the true posterior.  Thus, even though the posteriors for individual network parameters remain \textit{statistically} independent and can be sampled independently, they are \textit{functionally} coupled: if two network parameters must be high together to satisfy the data, optimization will push their means up together.  

Some methods employ variational specifications which specify parameter correlation structures, such as employing multivariate Normals with low-rank estimates of the covariance matrix \note{addref Ghosh and doshi-velez}, but this comes with computational cost.  Time complexity for training a network with $D$ weight parameters under the mean field assumptions is linear ($\mathcal{O}(D)$); for parameters modeled with a covariance matrix with rank $K$, that time complexity is $\mathcal{O}(DK^2)$.  
Furthermore, in the context of predictive uncertainty, even when the total dimension over layers is the same, deeper neural networks with fully factorized variational distributions have been shown to induce richer marginal distributions with complex covariance structures even than shallow networks with correlated weight posteriors \citep{farquhar_liberty_2021}.
