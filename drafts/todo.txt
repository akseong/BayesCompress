MFVI and variational distributions


Mean Field Variational Inference (MFVI) refers to variational optimization that adopts the Mean Field (MF) assumption that the joint posterior of the parameters is factorizable along a specified partition (i.e. that some partition of the parameters leads to independence between the partition's subsets), thus leading to simpler posteriors that are much easier to sample from and making computation tractable and highly scalable.  Even though the true joint posterior may be highly dependent, the individual variational posterior distributions remain independent; variational bayes procedures for this reason are known to underestimate variance, essentially approximating a highly correlated posterior with a product of independent "bubbles."

"The variational distribution will typically "under-fit" the true posterior, often becoming overly certain (picking the largest axis-aligned blob it can fit inside the true posterior)"
Christopher Bishop’s "Pattern Recognition and Machine Learning" (PRML), specifically in Section 10.1.2.


However, the variational approximation to the posterior remains useful.  Though the individual variational posteriors do not capture dependence in the joint posterior, the variational distributions' parameters are optimized \textit{jointly} to minimize the KL divergence to the true posterior.  Thus, even though the posteriors for individual network parameters remain \textit{statistically} independent and can be sampled independently, they are \textit{functionally} coupled: if two network parameters must be high together to satisfy the data, optimization will push their means up together.  

Some methods employ variational specifications which specify parameter correlation structures, such as employing multivariate Normals with low-rank estimates of the covariance matrix \note{addref Ghosh and doshi-velez}, but this comes with computational cost.  Time complexity for training a network with $D$ weight parameters under the mean field assumptions is linear ($\mathcal{O}(D)$); for parameters modeled with a covariance matrix with rank $K$, that time complexity is $\mathcal{O}(DK^2)$.  
Furthermore, in the context of predictive uncertainty, even when the total dimension over layers is the same, deeper neural networks with fully factorized variational distributions have been shown to induce richer marginal distributions with complex covariance structures even than shallow networks with correlated weight posteriors \citep{farquhar_liberty_2021}.

Title: Principled Sparsity in Flexible Models: Bayesian Variable Selection in Generalized Additive Models and Deep Neural Networks

Abstract:
Variable selection is the task of identifying the subset of predictors that are truly relevant to an outcome and represents a central challenge in modern statistics. Classical penalized-likelihood approaches such as the LASSO provide computationally efficient solutions in parametric settings, but face fundamental obstacles when the model space under scrutiny is large or the form of the relationships between predictors and response are not fully specified. This dissertation develops
Bayesian approaches to variable selection in two complementary semi-parametric settings. The first contribution addresses local variable selection in generalized additive models under possible model misspecification. We show that standard basis-expansion methods fail to achieve asymptotically consistent selection of localized functional effects, as the overlapping basis functions widely used in such analyses contaminate one another’s estimates. We resolve this by constructing an orthogonalized basis that eliminates basis overlap, and pair it with a model-complexity prior allowing us to frame the problem of local null testing and effect detection as a Bayesian variable selection problem. The resulting procedure attains selection consistency for localized effects and is shown to be robust under misspecification. The method is validated on wage data and electrocorticography (ECoG) recordings. The second contribution introduces a Bayesian neural network framework for variable selection based on the horseshoe prior. By embedding a continuous global-local shrinkage prior at the input layer of a deep network, the method inherits the expressive capacity of neural networks while retaining the interpretability and uncertainty quantification of principled Bayesian inference on variable selection. Simulation studies and real-data analyses demonstrate that the approach achieves sparse, stable input-variable selection even in the presence of complex, nonlinear response surfaces.





It is often of interest to know not only whether something has an effect, but also when and where the effect is present.  Testing of local effects, however, poses major methodological difficulties, particularly when using expressive models able to capture non-linear relationships.  In the first portion of this talk, we adopt the additive model framework, in which non-linear function spaces are linearized via basis expansion, and develop the concept of an orthogonal cut basis which yields consistent local null tests.  We formalize local null testing as Bayesian model selection with an orthogonal cut basis, applying the method to brain imaging data in order to describe neural responses to stimuli.  The second portion of this talk discusses ongoing work in applying Bayesian variable selection methods to deep neural networks, increasing parsimony and interpretability while preserving the expressivity afforded by deep neural networks.

\paragraph{Varying-coefficient extensions.}
The horseshoe BNN can be extended to the varying-coefficient setting
in the spirit of VC-BART \citep{deshpande:2024} by modeling responses
of the form
\begin{equation}
  y_i = \sum_{j=1}^{p} f_j(\bm{z}_i)\, x_{ij} + \varepsilon_i,
  \label{eq:ch3-vcm}
\end{equation}
where the effect of each predictor $x_j$ is modulated by an
auxiliary coordinate vector $\bm{z}_i$ (e.g., spatial or temporal
indices) through a smooth modulator $f_j(\cdot)$.  One natural
architecture fits a single horseshoe BNN that takes $\bm{z}_i$ as
input and outputs a vector
$(\hat{f}_1(\bm{z}_i), \ldots, \hat{f}_p(\bm{z}_i))^\top$, whose
final layer is then multiplied elementwise by $\bx_i$ under a
grouped horseshoe prior on the $\bx$-side of the product;
alternatively, each $f_j$ can be modelled by its own sub-network
with shared horseshoe-prior-based selection.  This extension would
bring the expressive capacity of deep networks to bear on problems
that have so far been the domain of tree-based varying-coefficient
methods \citep{deshpande:2024, ghosh_fitting_2025}.

- application of horseshoe BNN to other types of layers or in / alongside other neural network architectures.  E.G. incorporation of LSTMs for time series, application to convolutional filters for images.  



# Other approaches to variable selection in NNs:

\paragraph{Discrete spike-and-slab priors with normalizing flows.}
A qualitatively different avenue is to replace the continuous
horseshoe shrinkage with a discrete spike-and-slab prior on the
row-groups of $\bW^{(1)}$, using non-local slabs \citep{rossell:2017} to improve inclusion
probability separation, coupled with the concrete relaxation of the Bernoulli mixing parameter, and using normalizing flows \citep{huang_neural_2018}
as the variational family.  This would yield exact PIPs rather than
pseudo-PIPs at the cost of a more complex variational optimization.

\paragraph{Knockoff hybrids and variational dropout rates.}
The empirical Bayes perspective of \citet{kingma_variational_2015}
furnishes an alternative relevance summary: the inverse coefficient
of variation of the posterior local scale,
$\alpha_j = \mathbb{E}^2[\lambda_j^{(1)}]\,/\,\mathrm{Var}[\lambda_j^{(1)}]$,
can be interpreted as a per-input variational dropout rate.
Combined with a knockoff filter \citep{barber_controlling_2015,
candes_panning_2018}, this statistic could provide finite-sample FDR
control while sidestepping the $\tau^{(1)}$ corrections altogether,
at the cost of the assumptions on the covariate distribution
required by model-X knockoffs.
