# Sim results:


ss-gam takes too long.  

HS-2 outperforms HS-4 in terms of Sensitivity; HS2 picks up on more true positives, at the cost of a little predictive error.

SoftBART's sensitivity is consistently higher than HS2; f1 scores are modestly higher; but at a very large cost in predictive error.

HS-2 and HS-4's functional MSE also decreases with sample size; that does not occur with SoftBART.  If anything, it appears to increase.

HS2 and 4 have stable test MSE across $n = 1k, 2k, 5k$; few, if any false positives, well below the target BFDR; and learns from additional observations.


Our method effectively controls FDR, with exceedingly rare false positives, and excellent predictive performance as measured by the test MSE and functional MSE.  While SoftBART achieves higher sensitivity, its advantage diminishes with sample size, and it appears to have difficulty learning from more observations.


Each methods' test mse is relatively stable across sample sizes within each setting.  However, our method is the only one whose functional MSE decreases with sample size, indicating that the neural network is actually able to better approximate the truth.


Specificity: All methods' Specificity is perfect or near perfect, and all do a fairly good job of controlling FDR, with OLS linear regression the only method exceeding the nominal rate.
Sensitivity: The linear methods are at a severe handicap in Setting 1, as the functions in Setting 1 are largely symmetric; they perform better in Setting 2, where the interactions reduce symmetry, and perhaps also benefitting from the larger number of active variables.








\paragraph{Varying-coefficient extensions.}
The horseshoe BNN can be extended to the varying-coefficient setting
in the spirit of VC-BART \citep{deshpande:2024} by modeling responses
of the form
\begin{equation}
  y_i = \sum_{j=1}^{p} f_j(\bm{z}_i)\, x_{ij} + \varepsilon_i,
  \label{eq:ch3-vcm}
\end{equation}
where the effect of each predictor $x_j$ is modulated by an
auxiliary coordinate vector $\bm{z}_i$ (e.g., spatial or temporal
indices) through a smooth modulator $f_j(\cdot)$.  One natural
architecture fits a single horseshoe BNN that takes $\bm{z}_i$ as
input and outputs a vector
$(\hat{f}_1(\bm{z}_i), \ldots, \hat{f}_p(\bm{z}_i))^\top$, whose
final layer is then multiplied elementwise by $\bx_i$ under a
grouped horseshoe prior on the $\bx$-side of the product;
alternatively, each $f_j$ can be modelled by its own sub-network
with shared horseshoe-prior-based selection.  This extension would
bring the expressive capacity of deep networks to bear on problems
that have so far been the domain of tree-based varying-coefficient
methods \citep{deshpande:2024, ghosh_fitting_2025}.

\paragraph{Discrete spike-and-slab priors with normalizing flows.}
A qualitatively different avenue is to replace the continuous
horseshoe shrinkage with a discrete spike-and-slab prior on the
row-groups of $\bW^{(1)}$, in the spirit of
\citet{sun_learning_2022} and \citet{skaaret-lund_sparsifying_2023},
using non-local slabs \citep{rossell:2017} to improve inclusion
probability separation and normalizing flows \citep{huang_neural_2018}
as the variational family.  This would yield exact---rather than
pseudo---posterior inclusion probabilities at the cost of a more
complex variational optimization.

\paragraph{Knockoff hybrids and variational dropout rates.}
The empirical Bayes perspective of \citet{kingma_variational_2015}
furnishes an alternative relevance summary: the inverse coefficient
of variation of the posterior local scale,
$\alpha_j = \mathbb{E}^2[\lambda_j^{(1)}]\,/\,\mathrm{Var}[\lambda_j^{(1)}]$,
can be interpreted as a per-input variational dropout rate.
Combined with a knockoff filter \citep{barber_controlling_2015,
candes_panning_2018}, this statistic could provide finite-sample FDR
control while sidestepping the $\tau^{(1)}$ corrections altogether,
at the cost of the assumptions on the covariate distribution
required by model-X knockoffs.
